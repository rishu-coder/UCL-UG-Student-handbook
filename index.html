2. Being a Civil Engineer

Civil engineering is the art and practice of changing the physical world for the use and benefit of people. It is one of the oldest professions in the world, borne of people's enduring relationship with their environment and the need to sustain and improve life. Within the context of our complex and diverse world in the 21st Century, it is achieved with a rich social, cultural and technological heritage.

As every person is different, so is every civil engineer. As an individual you can play your part in designing and building bridges, skyscrapers, flood defences, water supply, sustainable buildings, train stations, ports, tunnels and roads. There is no "one size fits all", and there is a place at UCL to learn how your own interests can find a home within the breadth of civil engineering.

Some people relish the generalist's challenge to develop the combination of design, technical, leadership and management skills to lead complex multi-disciplinary projects. Others take great pleasure in developing their specialist knowledge in a particular area of interest, often going on to become highly skilled in analysis or research techniques, sometimes in a single technical domain. It takes both sorts of engineer, and others from a range of disciplines, to deliver a successful civil engineering project. That is because all civil engineering projects are the product of multi-disciplinary teams; the best projects are designed and built by teams with well-integrated complementary skills.

Successful engineers often say that one of the best things about their career is that they never stop learning, combining practical skills with an understanding of the ever-changing financial, sociological and political frameworks in which they operate. As civil engineers, we work on projects that impact on people's everyday lives across the whole planet. That is no small responsibility, and learning to be a good civil engineer brings the great satisfaction of taking that responsibility and turning it into something that really does make the world a better place.

As a discipline, civil engineering needs diverse viewpoints and skillsets to successfully identify and address the complexities and challenges of 21st Century living. Strengths in collaboration, creativity and communication are as critical to our field as technical expertise in the mathematical and physical sciences. In CEGE you will find a diverse staff from a wide range of disciplines and backgrounds; a community here to enrich, enable and support you in your own learning journey within civil engineering.

4. Programme Overview

Our programmes aim to produce engineers with good problem-solving abilities, excellent communication and team working skills, who are capable of managing the design or construction of large engineering projects, and are comfortable taking on complex interdisciplinary challenges.

A large proportion of teaching and learning is done through projects, so you have many opportunities to put your engineering skills into practice during your studies. Our programmes place particular emphasis on people and the environment, because civil engineers design and construct projects which ultimately impact on people and our environment. In addition to developing your theoretical, technical, and design abilities, we also focus on developing the communication, management, team working and other skills needed to be a successful engineer, all emphasized through group work.

This innovative approach to teaching is what makes our degree at UCL world leading.

You are taught by a team of academic staff from around the world, who are leading researchers in their field or specialists in teaching. Professional engineers from leading engineering firms also teach and mentor on our degrees. All students have a personal tutor who they meet regularly to discuss their studies, personal development and wellbeing.

4.2 MEng Engineering (Civil – International Programme)

There is an opportunity to spend your 3rd year of study abroad at a host university. We hold information sessions in Year 1 and Year 2 to ensure you do not miss out if you are interested. The study abroad programme is an exciting opportunity to experience other cultures, adopt new ways of learning and develop broader horizons.

UCL has exchange schemes with universities in Europe, North America, Japan, Hong Kong, Singapore and Australia. The list of partner universities and the number of places available varies every year – check the UCL Study Abroad website for up-to-date information. UCL cannot guarantee that any student will get a place on their first choice of destination. However, past experience suggests that all students who have been accepted on the programme are offered a suitable place in alternative destinations. For those wanting to attend a non-English language university, you will need to demonstrate capability (minimum GCSE level) in the relevant language. In addition, it may be appropriate for you to pick Modern Foreign Languages as your Minor.

Placements on the Study Abroad programme are highly selective and the selection process is normally based on your first year results, a personal statement and a short interview. Your overall results must be greater than 65% in Year 1, preferably 70%, to be accepted onto the programme. If you wish to go on the year abroad programme you must be enrolled on the MEng Engineering (Civil – International Programme) (H205). Applications will not be considered if you are not registered on the year abroad programme by the start of Reading Week of Term 1 in Year 2. The Study Abroad tutors reserve the right to withdraw the offer of a place to a student who does not maintain consistently high results in Year 2. While abroad, the students will remain in touch with the Study Abroad Tutors at CEGE who act as their personal tutors for the year. As well as exciting, the programme can be challenging, but past students have found it to be extremely rewarding and career-enhancing.

For more information about Study Abroad, please contact the Study Abroad Tutor, Dr Rodolfo Lorenzo: r.lorenzo@ucl.ac.uk

4.3 BEng/MEng Engineering (Civil – Year in Industry)

You can opt to take a year out in industry before your final year of study. At the end of your work placement you will need to produce a portfolio that outlines the activities that you carried out. This report and the year in industry do not count towards your degree classification but your degree certificate will show "BEng or MEng with year in Industry". Should you wish to follow this route, we will support you but it is your responsibility to find a suitable placement. UCL will not charge tuition fees during your year in industry.

If you are interested in taking a year out in industry, please speak to Prof Aiduan Borrion (the Year in Industry Tutor): a.borrion@ucl.ac.uk and read the information on the student information page: CEGE Student Intranet.

4.5 Rules for Progression and Scheme of Award

Rules for progression and award on degree programmes in the department comply with the regulations as set out in Chapter 4: Assessment Framework for Taught Programmes of the UCL Academic Manual. However, there are local variations to these for Engineering programmes.

In order to comply with the Engineering Council requirements for professional accreditation, this programme is subject to condonement regulations which differ from the standard UCL regulations in the following ways:

  1. Condonement applies only to module marks falling within the following Condonable Ranges:
    • Modules at level 4, 5 and 6: 30.00% – 39.99%
    • Modules at level 7: 40.00% – 49.99%
  2. In order to progress from one year to the next, or to be awarded a BEng or MEng with Honours, a student should Pass 120 credits in each year of study.
  3. A student who does not Pass 120 credits in a year of study must nonetheless be considered to have met the Progression and Award Requirements if they meet all of the following Condonement Criteria in that year of study:
    1. Pass all Non-Condonable Modules, and
    2. Pass at least 90 credits, and
    3. Attain marks in the Condonable Range in no more than 30 credits, and
    4. Have attained marks in the Condonable Range in no more than 30 credits up to and including the current year of study, and
    5. Have no marks below the condonable range, and
    6. Achieve a credit-weighted mean of at least 40.00% (50.00% in the Masters year) across 120 credits.
  4. At most 30 credits may be condoned in any given year of study AND at most 30 credits may be condoned across the programme as a whole.
  5. Cognate, non-accredited programmes have been established as exit points only (interim qualifications), with titles such as MEng/BEng Engineering Sciences (DISCIPLINE NAME). These programmes are designed for students who do not meet the previously outlined requirements but fulfil UCL's conditions for award.
MEng Progression Requirement: In addition to the requirements above, to progress from Year 2 to Year 3 on the MEng programme you must have a weighted average of at least 55.00% at the end of Year 2. If you fail to achieve this average but meet the remaining progression criteria you may progress to Year 3 but you will be transferred to the corresponding BEng programme. Subsequent transfer back to the MEng will not be possible.

In the event that you have not passed sufficient number of credits or failed a non-condonable module you will be required to retake the failed components of this module(s) during the Late Summer Assessment period. This is usually held in late August / early September and may include in-person examinations during this time.

5. Teaching & Learning Activities

5.1 Structure of Programme

In Years 1 and 2, you learn through a range of teaching and learning activities including lectures, clinics, problem classes, laboratories, exams, project work, site visits and field trips. Residential field trips (surveying and construction field courses), are among the highlights of the first and second years.

Challenges and Scenarios are authentic, problem-based learning projects where first, second- and third-year students work in teams on realistic engineering design projects (e.g. street renewal, sustainable building, etc). You apply, integrate and consolidate the knowledge acquired on the programme and elsewhere to solve complex problems that an engineer encounters in professional practice.

Year 3 Overview

Your third year comprises compulsory modules in civil engineering, including Civil Engineering in Practice, a module that looks at structural design to codes, project finance and management and also includes the Construction Field Course and a scenario. The Civil Engineering Project is your opportunity to undertake academic research under the supervision of academic staff. Here, students either propose their own projects or select projects of interest from a published list. A broad array of topics is available from across the civil engineering spectrum and can be lab-based, desk-based or field-based. The project supervisors will also act as personal tutors throughout Year 3. You will meet with your supervisors regularly and receive help on report writing, but self-management is also critical to the project's success. You will need to submit interim and final submissions as well as present your projects to your supervisor and second marker.

Year 4 (MEng Only) Overview

In the fourth year, all MEng students undertake an Integrated Design Project. This module is the capstone of your MEng degree. This is a large-scale, industry-facing design exercise, running over the year, where industry partners take a leading role to ensure the experience mirrors a real civil engineering project, from bidding for a project to the delivery of a final design. Past projects include: a skyscraper in Waterloo, a pier in Monaco, flood defences in Genoa, a new port facility in Angola, an extension to the Docklands Light Railway (DLR) in East London, a 'Grey Belt' site development with sustainable housing in London, and a multi-use offshore renewable energy demonstration scheme in the UK.

Students also select four advanced modules, drawn from two streams. There is also the possibility to enrol on any UCL Level 7 modules for which you are eligible but please check this directly with the relevant home department via the Module Leader listed in the UCL Module Catalogue.

5.3 Workload and Time Management

The workload for students including private study is approximately 40 hours per week. You should be prepared to spend more time on your studies in a given week if necessary. It is very important that you plan ahead and manage your time effectively.

You are expected to attend timetabled learning events and to conduct adequate personal study activities in your own time.

Lectures and coursework are closely linked so a good understanding of the lecture contents will help you with your coursework. It is recommended you are proactive with this and keep on top of your lectures and additional reading.

You should attend all lectures and ensure that you fully understand all aspects of each lecture in advance of the next one. Lectures provide a basic framework for a course, but you should also complement your learning through background reading, problem sheets and group discussions.

You must submit all your coursework. Students who do not submit any component will be awarded a mark of 0% for that component.

6.3 Personal Tutorials

Every student is assigned a personal tutor. This is a member of academic staff who will work closely and informally with you over the year to support your learning and engagement with the programme.

At least four personal tutorial sessions will be timetabled per year. The responsibility of scheduling these meetings sits with the Personal Tutor; the responsibility of attending these meetings sits with you. These sessions provide an opportunity to get to know a member of staff to act as the first point of contact if you have personal or academic issues (see also 6.7 and 6.8). Your personal tutor can also write reference letters for you (for academic and non-academic applications).

Personal Tutor should arrange a professional visit to a construction site and/or a consulting engineering firm. These are designed to enhance awareness of professional engineering practice and to inspire you in your studies and future careers.

6.4 Additional Maths Sessions

We arrange additional maths sessions to support students who are finding maths particularly difficult. If you were admitted without an A-level in Maths or are struggling with the 'Mathematical Modelling & Analysis 1' module, please discuss this with your Personal Tutor or speak to the Year 1 Co-ordinator Dr Pippa Cowles.

6.5 Additional English Sessions

The working language of UCL is English, and proficiency in written and spoken English is essential in order to make the most of your time at UCL. The UCL Academic Communication Centre (ACC) supports UCL students to develop their academic language and literacies. They assist students of all language backgrounds, across faculties, at all levels of study, to communicate more effectively in their discipline. They work closely with UCL academic departments and faculties to develop tailored support.

Their website is here: UCL Academic Communication Centre

In Year 1 we identify students who are having difficulty writing in English, and can arrange additional support. If you feel your English language skills are detracting from your academic performance, please discuss this with your Personal Tutor or the first year coordinator.

6.6 Transition Mentors

The UCL Transition Programme supports new first-year students at UCL, helping them to settle in quickly and achieve their potential. Each first-year student is assigned a Transition Mentor for their first term. Transition mentors are later-year students within each department who work with small groups of students on a weekly basis to help them settle in to UCL and London as well as focussing on academic issues and topics specific to their degree programme. First-year students meet their Transition Mentor during the first week of term at their department's 'Meet your Mentor' session.

Further information: Transition Mentors | UCL

6.7 Academic Issues

Please follow the protocol below to address academic issues (i.e. those relating to a particular module, piece of assessment etc.) as they arise:

  1. Approach the member of staff directly, i.e. try to resolve the problem informally first.
  2. Discuss with your Personal Tutor, who can provide advice or attempt to resolve the problem.
  3. Mention it to your Student Representative, who meets regularly with the Year Coordinator and represents your class at the Departmental Partnership Committee. Others may be experiencing the same difficulty.
  4. Speak to the relevant Year Coordinator.
  5. Speak to the Undergraduate Programme Director.
  6. Send an email to cege-feedback@ucl.ac.uk – this is anonymous and all identifying information will be removed before the email is passed to the Director of Studies, who will speak with the relevant Section Head and Head of Department to resolve the issue.
  7. Speak to the Director of Studies.
  8. Speak to the Head of Department.

6.8 Personal Issues

If you are experiencing personal issues that may impact on your academic performance, please follow the protocol below:

  1. Discuss with your Personal Tutor or Student Adviser, who can provide advice or point you towards someone who can assist (NB. You can also approach any member of staff with whom you feel comfortable talking).
  2. You can read about the range of support services UCL provides for students online: www.ucl.ac.uk/current-students/support
  3. Speak to the Departmental UG Pastoral Tutor, especially if you need to tell us about extenuating circumstances (personal or health issues affecting your work) or wish to interrupt your studies. The Departmental UG Pastoral Tutor can also advise on Authorised Absences (Tier 4) and Withdrawals.
  4. Speak with the Director of Studies.
If you need assistance, please don't be afraid to ask for it. Everyone experiences personal difficulties at some stage during their lives. We will try to give assistance wherever we can to ensure your experience at UCL is fulfilling, healthy and enjoyable.

8. Professionalism

8.1 Professional Charter

As Engineers we have a responsibility to be professional in carrying out our work. This means treating the people you work with and your environment with respect.

We expect you to:

  • Act in the manner expected of a professional engineer
  • Carry out your studies effectively, to a good standard and within prescribed timetabled periods
  • Respect your fellow students, staff, others, and your environment
  • Submit your coursework on time
  • Bring any problems to the attention of your Personal Tutor, UG Pastoral Tutor, Student Adviser, Year Coordinator, Undergraduate Programme Director or Director of Studies, including those of a medical or social nature, and especially if it is affecting your work
  • Speak in confidence to the Director of Studies or Faculty Tutor if you feel that there is a problem with your programme that has not been resolved by the mechanisms described here
  • Follow the procedures set out for study leave or interruption of study

You can expect us to:

  • Act in the manner expected of a professional engineer
  • Provide support for your learning that is well organised
  • Be sympathetic and take seriously any concerns or problems that you raise regarding your programme or your personal circumstances
  • Provide written or verbal feedback on all coursework within four weeks of submission, in accordance with UCL guidelines
  • Provide access to and advice on the support services offered by UCL
  • Give opportunities for feedback on all modules and programmes and demonstrate the results of this feedback
  • Provide a personal tutor who can support your professional development

8.3 Becoming a Chartered Engineer

Chartered engineers have been assessed by their peers as competent professionals in their field. It is a registered professional title (CEng) which is internationally recognised, and certain engineering work is reserved for chartered engineers.

To become a chartered engineer, you first need to satisfy the educational base, i.e. complete an accredited degree. After graduating from university you continue to practise and develop your professional skills and competencies whilst working as an engineer. After you have gained sufficient experience, typically after you have completed your graduate training programme, you can become a chartered engineer by demonstrating these skills and competencies in a professional ICE exam.

Our undergraduate degree programmes offer a first step towards becoming a chartered engineer and are accredited by The Joint Board of Moderators (JBM).

  • The MEng in Civil Engineering is accredited as fully satisfying the educational base to become a chartered engineer (CEng) and provides a more direct route to becoming chartered.
  • The BEng in Civil Engineering partially satisfies the educational base for a Chartered Engineer, meaning that a programme of accredited further learning (e.g. UCL MSc Civil Engineering or MSc Earthquake Engineering with Disaster Management) is required to complete the education base to become a chartered engineer.

8.4 Professional Institutions

Professional institutions are centres of engineering knowledge which represent engineers and promote their positive impact on society. They ensure engineers are properly qualified, through accrediting degree programmes, and managing the process for becoming a Chartered Engineer.

Most institutions offer free student membership, which includes access to lectures on exciting engineering projects, and access to their libraries and publications. It is a good idea to join the Institution of Civil Engineers (ICE) initially, and then other institutions as your interests develop. Mentioning these memberships on your CV shows potential employers that you are thinking seriously about your future career.

9. Understanding Your Marks

The UK university undergraduate marking system has historically been different from most other systems around the world. The system is also likely to differ from what you are used to from school or college.

Marking guidance will be provided for all assignments. Please see the CEGE Departmental Manual for All Students for general marking guidelines. Where marking guidance is missing, please contact the Module Lead in the first instance, then the Director of Studies for follow-up.

9.1 Honours Degree

If you study for an undergraduate degree at UCL, you are aiming to graduate with a degree with honours. With this type of degree, the level of your academic performance on your programme is indicated by the 'class' of degree – or honours – you are awarded. Most universities award a class of degree based on the marks from the assessed work you have completed. To distinguish between students on the basis of their academic achievement, undergraduate degree awards are classified as follows:

First Class
70% and above

A first-class degree, usually referred to as a 'first' or 1st, is the highest honours degree you can achieve.

Upper Second Class
60–69%

Known as a 2:1 or two-one, is the higher of the two levels of second-class degree.

Lower Second Class
50–59%

A 2.2 or two-two is the lower level of the second-class degree.

Third Class
40–49%

Known as a 'third' or 3rd, this degree is the lowest honours degree achievable.

Ordinary Degree
Below threshold

If an honours student fails to achieve a third class by a small margin, they will be awarded an ordinary degree i.e. without honours.

9.2 BA, BSc, MEng or what?

When you graduate, the honours degree you are awarded depends on the disciplines you study.

  • For example, if you take an undergraduate degree in biochemistry, the qualification you will receive is BSc, where 'B' signifies 'Bachelor' and 'Sc' signifies 'Science'.
  • If you take an integrated Master's degree (a four year honours degree course which combines three years of undergraduate study with a fourth year at postgraduate level) the qualification you will receive is MEng, MSci or MPharm, depending on your discipline.

In CEGE, at the successful completion of your UCL taught undergraduate programme, you will be awarded one of the following degrees, according to your discipline:

  • Bachelor of Engineering (BEng)
  • Master of Engineering (MEng)* — integrated Master's degree

Other interim qualifications will be available as outlined earlier in Section 4.8.

9.3 Assessment at UCL

The way your assessments are marked is likely to be different to your previous school or college. Your module marks will be out of 100 and given to 2 decimal places. Marking guidelines (e.g. criteria, grade descriptors etc.) are provided at departmental level or provided for individual assignments; please check with the person setting the assignment if you are unsure. Please also see the UCL Student Guide to Assessment and Feedback.

9.4 What is your assessor looking for?

UCL's Academic Manual states that to successfully complete your undergraduate degree at UCL, you will have to demonstrate:

  • A systematic understanding of key aspects of your field of study, including coherent and detailed knowledge, at least some of which is at, or informed by, the forefront of your field of study
  • An ability to use accurately the established techniques of analysis and enquiry within your field of study
  • A conceptual understanding that enables you to devise and sustain arguments and solve problems, using ideas and techniques, some of which are at the forefront of your field of study
  • A conceptual understanding that means you can describe and comment on aspects of current research in the field of study
  • An appreciation of the uncertainty, ambiguity and limits of knowledge
  • An ability to manage your own learning
  • An ability to make use of scholarly reviews and primary sources

Professional institutions will have different or additional learning outcomes.
